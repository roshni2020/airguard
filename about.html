Why

Driverless baggage tractors already operate beside aircraft at Singapore Changi and Dubai DWC, with trials at Schiphol and Stuttgart. Their driving software is updated often, increasingly by AI. Nobody independently proves an update is safe before it reaches the fleet.

How it works

1. An AI writes an updateMeta Muse Spark turns a change request ("make the route 20% faster") into new controller code.
2. SubQ red-teams itSubQ reads the full FAA AC 150/5210-20A, the real SFO map, the code and every earlier simulation trace (up to 676k tokens in one call), then designs harder scenarios each round.
3. Python judgesA deterministic simulator on real SFO geometry runs every scenario. Fixed FAA-cited checks decide PASS or BLOCK. No model decides.
4. Block or deployUnsafe means blocked and rolled back, with a phone call to ops. Safe means deployed to the fleet. Every verdict is hashed and reproducible.

What it found

Across live runs SubQ found real bugs that hand-written tests missed: a tractor run over by a pushback while waiting, one too slow to leave a jet-blast zone when a beacon lit, debris blocking the route, and a 3.47 m crew near-miss against a 3.5 m limit. It also found a jet-blast flaw in the already approved controller. On mutation testing, SubQ's scenarios raise the catch rate from 56% (built-in tests) to 89% with 0 false blocks. A diff-only AI review rated the genuinely safe fix as unsafe (P(safe) = 0.055).

Real vs simulated

Real: SFO airport geometry (OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL), FAA AC 150/5210-20A rules and citations, and aircraft dimensions (FAA Aircraft Characteristics Database). Approximate: engine positions and jet-blast lengths. Example values: speed limits and clearances (the FAA leaves them to the airport operator). Simulated: tractors, aircraft schedules, crew, fog and GPS noise.
